Turkish people are famously hospitable, and most are pleased to see visitors taking an interest in their country. A little knowledge of local customs goes a long way, especially when you visit mosques or are invited into someone's home.

## Greetings and conversation

A handshake is the usual greeting. Some people, particularly more religious men and women, prefer not to shake hands with the opposite sex; if someone doesn't offer a hand, a nod and a smile is fine. Close friends and family greet each other with a kiss on both cheeks.

A few words of Turkish are always appreciated: *merhaba* (hello), *teşekkür ederim* (thank you), *lütfen* (please) and *kolay gelsin* (literally "may it come easy", said to someone working).

Turkey's founder, **Mustafa Kemal Atatürk**, is deeply respected, and insulting his memory or the Turkish flag is a criminal offence. Politics and religion are sensitive subjects to approach carefully with people you've just met.

## Visiting mosques

Mosques are working places of worship that welcome respectful visitors.

- **Dress.** Cover your shoulders and knees. Women should cover their hair; many mosques lend scarves and wraps at the entrance, but it's easier to bring your own.
- **Shoes.** Take your shoes off before stepping onto the carpet, and carry them in the bags provided or leave them on the racks.
- **Prayer times.** Visit outside prayer times. Mosques close to visitors during the five daily prayers, and for longer around the Friday midday prayer.
- **Inside.** Be quiet, don't walk in front of people who are praying, and avoid photographing worshippers. Use the visitor entrance where one is signposted.

## Dress in general

In cities and resorts, dress is relaxed and Western clothing is the norm. In conservative areas, rural villages and smaller towns, dress more modestly and avoid very short shorts or beachwear away from the beach. Topless sunbathing isn't generally accepted.

## Hospitality, food and drink

If you're offered tea (*çay*), accept: it's a gesture of friendship, and shopkeepers often offer it while you browse. If you're invited to someone's home, take your shoes off at the door, and bring a small gift such as sweets or pastries. Your host may insist on paying in a restaurant; offering to pay is polite, but don't argue for too long.

Alcohol is legal and widely available in cities and tourist areas, though some restaurants, especially in conservative areas, don't serve it. Drinking in public and drunkenness are frowned upon.

## Ramadan

During **Ramadan** (*Ramazan*), many Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. Restaurants, cafés and tourist services stay open, especially in cities and resorts. Visitors aren't expected to fast, but it's considerate not to eat, drink or smoke conspicuously in public in more conservative areas. Evenings are lively, with special *iftar* meals to break the fast. Ramadan ends with the three-day **Ramazan Bayramı** holiday, when transport and hotels are very busy.

## Other courtesies

- **Ask before photographing people**, especially women in rural areas.
- **Public displays of affection** beyond holding hands are best avoided outside the big cities.
- **Mind your feet.** Don't point the soles of your feet at people, or put your feet on furniture.
- **Bargain with good humour** in bazaars, but not in shops with fixed prices.
